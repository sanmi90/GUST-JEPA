Small uncrewed aerial vehicles are increasingly expected to operate in
environments where the incoming flow is neither steady nor weakly disturbed.
Urban canyons, mountainous terrain, coastal regions and ship or building wakes
can expose these vehicles to gusts whose characteristic length scale is
comparable to the wing chord and whose velocity is of the same order as the
flight speed \citep{jones2022,mohamed2023gusts,fukami2023}. In this regime,
gusts cannot be treated as small perturbations of an otherwise steady
aerodynamic state. They interact with separation, vortex shedding and wake
recovery, producing large transient loads and rapid changes in the surrounding
flow organisation. For small vehicles, this coupling directly affects
navigability, control authority and flight safety.

This regime has motivated a growing body of work on extreme aerodynamics
\citep{taira2026extreme}. A canonical example is the interaction between a
discrete vortex gust and a wing or airfoil, where the disturbance has
sufficient strength to reorganise the near wake rather than merely modulate the
instantaneous force \citep{fukami2025prf}. Recent studies have shown that
apparently complex gust encounters can exhibit unexpectedly low-dimensional
structure when viewed in suitable coordinates. Lift-augmented autoencoders have
revealed compact manifolds for extreme vortex-gust airfoil interactions
\citep{fukami2023}, and the same reduced description has been used to support
phase-amplitude analysis and data-driven transient lift attenuation
\citep{fukami2024control}. Related work has also shown that large-scale
vortical structures can display similarities across laminar and turbulent
extreme-gust encounters \citep{odaka2026}. These results suggest that the
dynamical organisation of extreme aerodynamic flows may be simpler than the
full flow-field dimension implies.

For practical deployment, however, compactness alone is not sufficient. A
reduced description intended for gust mitigation must be usable as a plant
state: it must be estimated from available sensors, advanced in time by a
predictive model and queried by a controller or decision algorithm
\citep{loiseau2018}. This requirement places the problem closer to state
estimation and model-based control than to flow-field compression alone.
Surface pressure is especially important in this context, because it is one of
the few measurements that can be obtained at high bandwidth on an aerodynamic
body. Recent gust-modelling studies have therefore used sparse surface-pressure
histories to infer unsteady aerodynamic loads and, more broadly, to bridge
spatial pressure correlations and temporal flow memory
\citep{zhong2023sparse,haughn2024deep,chen2026bridging}, and Bayesian sequential estimation has
begun to close the loop between sparse pressure and low-order aerodynamic
states, with uncertainty carried alongside the estimate
\citep{mousavi2025,eldredge2025guide}. Such approaches indicate a path towards
deployable gust-response models, but they also sharpen the central question:
what reduced state should be estimated from the wall in the first place?

Most current nonlinear reduced-order models for extreme aerodynamic flows are
built around reconstruction-trained autoencoders. In this approach, a
convolutional encoder maps the flow field to a low-dimensional latent vector,
and a decoder reconstructs the original field. Aerodynamic observables, such as
lift, can be added as auxiliary losses to orient the latent space towards
physically relevant quantities \citep{fukami2023,fukami2025}. This strategy is
powerful because it preserves a direct connection between the latent variables
and the resolved flow. It also provides a natural route to visualisation and
interpretation, since the decoder maps latent coordinates back to physical
space. Similar ideas have been combined with variational autoencoders and
sequence models to build predictive reduced-order models of chaotic flows
\citep{solera2024,fukagata2025compressing}.

Yet a reconstruction-trained latent space is not necessarily the best space for
prediction. The decoder encourages the encoder to preserve information needed
to reproduce the instantaneous field, but the time-advancing model requires
coordinates that remain stable and informative under autoregressive rollout.
These two requirements need not coincide. Recent comparisons in controlled wake
flows indicate that convolutional-autoencoder-based predictors can be more
prone to long-horizon divergence, whereas POD-based predictors, despite their
linear and less compact representation, may retain higher median forecast
accuracy and a narrower spread \citep{solerarico_compactness_underreview}. This
does not make POD preferable as a general representation of nonlinear separated
flows. Rather, it exposes a tension: nonlinear encoders can compress more
aggressively, but reconstruction compactness does not guarantee forecast
robustness. The question is therefore whether one can construct nonlinear
reduced states that retain the compactness of learned manifolds while improving
their predictive usability.

This question motivates the use of a joint-embedding predictive architecture
(JEPA) \citep{lecun2022}. In contrast with an autoencoder, a JEPA does not
train the representation by reconstructing the input field. Instead, it learns
an encoder and a predictor such that the present latent state predicts a future
latent state. In computer vision, image- and video-based JEPAs have been
introduced as non-generative self-supervised models that predict in
representation space rather than pixel space \citep{ijepa,vjepa2}. For the
present fluid-mechanics problem, the relevance of JEPA is more specific: it
provides a way to train the reduced coordinates in the same space in which the
forward model will later operate. The architecture is therefore used here as a
diagnostic tool to test whether predictive training can produce a nonlinear
state that is more stable under rollout than a reconstruction-trained latent.

This choice creates a second question. Because the JEPA state is trained
without a decoder, it is not obvious what physical information is retained by
its coordinates. A low forecast error in latent space would be insufficient if
the state did not carry the wake variables that matter for gust response. We
therefore treat decoding not as part of the training objective, but as an
a-posteriori diagnostic. After training the reduced state, we ask what can be
recovered from it: aerodynamic observables, wake enstrophy, flow-field
structures and, ultimately, the latent coordinates themselves from sparse wall
pressure. This separation between predictive training and diagnostic decoding
allows us to distinguish three properties that are often conflated: whether a
state is compact, whether it is physically readable and whether it remains
usable when advanced by its own dynamics.

The present work examines these questions using a canonical post-stall
vortex-gust interaction. The gust parameters are not supplied to the predictive
model; the encoder receives only the instantaneous flow state, and the
predictor advances the latent state from its own history. We compare a
predictive joint-embedding model with reconstruction-trained nonlinear encoders
and a linear POD representation at matched latent dimension. The comparison is
not designed to identify the method that reconstructs the vorticity field most
accurately. Instead, it asks which representation supplies a reduced
aerodynamic state that can be forecast, decoded and estimated in a form
relevant to future gust-mitigation systems.

The paper makes three contributions. First, we pose the extreme vortex-gust
encounter as a predictive-state estimation problem and fix its endpoints in
advance: the reduced state must carry the post-impact wake response, summarised
by the wake enstrophy and complementary flow diagnostics that scalar force
histories alone can miss, and it must be recoverable from wall pressure and
advanced by a forward model without leaving the region on which that recovery
was fitted. Second, we separate the ingredients of such a state with matched
controls, and reach a division of labour. Observable supervision on the wake
supplies both its instantaneous readability and an anisotropic latent geometry
into which the forward rollout settles, where an anti-collapse regulariser
applied to a bare reconstruction leaves the latent near-isotropic and its
rollout departs along low-variance directions; the multi-step predictive
objective supplies the long-horizon rollout stability that neither a
single-step objective nor an objective-free supervised encoder provides. Latent
drift is the mechanism through which an apparently accurate reconstructive
state loses this physics over the rollout. Third, we demonstrate the state in a
leakage-free sequential filter that senses only wall pressure and never
observes the vorticity field, and we map the gust-intensity envelope over which
it holds. The filter tracks the transient load through the encounter and
extends the usable gust range beyond what a single pressure frame can invert,
and a delay-coordinate reading explains why: the encounter is low-dimensional,
so a short window of pressure reconstructs the state a single frame cannot, and
the envelope closes where the strengthening gust drives the interaction
three-dimensional and its effective dimension outruns a fixed sensing budget.
We are explicit about the limits: the state's recoverability margin over a raw
field latent is modest, and the filter is under-dispersed at the strongest
gusts. This study investigates a fluid-mechanics question through a learning
architecture: what kind of reduced state can carry the wake dynamics of an
extreme gust encounter while remaining estimable from the wall and predictive
enough to serve as a future plant model?
