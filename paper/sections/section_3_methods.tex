\subsection{Model and training objective}
\label{sec:methods_arch}
\label{sec:methods_loss}

\rb{K}{S3-01}{The encoder and the justification of $d = 32$ as a deployment criterion rather than a tuned choice. Referees will look for exactly this.}The model takes no gust parameters: neither the encoder nor the predictor receives $(G, D, Y)$. The predictive encoder $E_\theta$ maps the mid-plane vorticity field $\omegaz(192,96)$ to a latent $\zvec \in \mathbb{R}^d$ at the headline dimension $d = \LatentDim$. This dimension is a deployment criterion rather than an accuracy-tuned choice, and the criterion is the sensing budget the estimator has to work with. Reading $K$ wall-pressure taps over a window of $m$ delays gives $Km$ scalar measurements per update, and the delay-embedding bound of \S\ref{sec:methods_estimator} holds a state of dimension $d$ to be generically reconstructible from them once $Km > 2d$. The production budget of \S\ref{sec:methods_estimator} is $K = \FilterTaps$ taps over a ten-frame window, which admits $d$ up to $40$; $d = \LatentDim$ sits just inside that ceiling, so the state is as large as the wall can support and no larger. The representation results below confirm it also sits on a readability plateau. The encoder is a hybrid network (figure~\ref{fig:method}a): three convolutional downsampling stages produce a $24 \times 12 \times 256$ feature map, a six-layer vision transformer processes the resulting tokens, and the class-token output is projected to $\zvec$ through a single linear layer with a batch-normalisation boundary, which the characteristic-function regulariser below requires. Layer counts, widths and parameter counts for the encoder and the predictor are in table~\ref{tab:architecture} (appendix~\ref{app:methods_tables}). The encoder is therefore a pure state map, and this is the property that lets the latent itself serve as the observable a controller would have access to, since at deployment the gust parameters are not known a priori (\S\ref{sec:discussion}).\re

\rb{K}{S3-02}{Carries equation (1), the one statement of the forecasting problem. The two-network description duplicates APA-01, which is where the detail belongs.}The predictor $P_\phi$ advances the pooled state in time, from its own history alone, so the model is unconditioned end to end. Throughout the paper the forecasting task is one map, from a context of $L$ past states to the future latent sequence over a horizon $H$,
\begin{equation}
  [\hat{\zvec}_{t+1}, \dots, \hat{\zvec}_{t+H}]
  = f\!\left(\zvec_{t-L+1}, \dots, \zvec_{t}\right),
  \label{eq:forecast}
\end{equation}
with no gust parameters among the inputs; what varies between models is how $f$ is realised. The training predictor is a recursive single-step model rather than a sequence-to-sequence one: a six-layer autoregressive transformer with rotary temporal positions and a causal mask maps a short history of $32$-vectors to the next $32$-vector alone, and the horizon is covered by feeding that prediction back in as the newest history element and repeating, so its errors can compound along the horizon. The one-shot alternative, which emits all $H$ future states from one forward pass and so cannot compound, is the direct multi-horizon forecaster of \S\ref{sec:methods_rex}; the two are compared in \S\ref{sec:res_forecast}. During training it rolls eight steps open loop from the two most recent encoded states, and the loss is the multi-step error between the rolled coefficients and the online encoder outputs at the matching frames, detached. The pipeline is therefore as plain as a linear or autoencoder reduced-order model: $32$ numbers in, a dynamics model on the vector, $32$ numbers out, with no spatial map anywhere in the loop. The state is the pooled vector at every stage: the anti-collapse regulariser, the observable heads, the probes, and the estimator all read the same $32$-vector, and nothing in the model carries more. The estimator of \S\ref{sec:methods_estimator}, an ensemble Kalman filter that advances a cloud of candidate states through a prediction model and corrects them against sparse wall-pressure measurements at each frame, refits a predictor of this same class on the frozen pooled trajectories for its predict step, so the training and estimation dynamics share a function class. Figure~\ref{fig:method}(a) shows the encoder and predictor, and panel~(b) the deployment predict-correct loop; the matched evaluation protocol used throughout is figure~\ref{fig:eval_protocol} in appendix~\ref{app:reg}, which also draws the contrast between the predictive training signal, a latent-space error against a shared-weight target embedding, and the reconstructive one, a field-space error through a decoder (figure~\ref{fig:predictive_vs_reconstructive}). Full configuration, widths and parameter counts for both networks are in table~\ref{tab:architecture} in appendix~\ref{app:methods_tables}.\re

\rb{K}{S3-03}{The training objective and the anti-collapse term. Names the direct-forecaster-coupled lineage used at low dimension.}The encoder and the predictor are trained jointly on two terms. The first is a multi-step rollout error. From the two most recent encoded states the predictor is rolled eight steps forward on its own outputs, never being handed the true state at any intermediate step, and the eight predicted latents are compared with the latents the encoder produces from the corresponding true fields. Those targets are computed by the same encoder as it trains, not by a separate frozen copy, but the gradient is blocked from flowing back through them, so the encoder is optimised to make its own trajectory predictable rather than to move the target towards the prediction. The second term exists because that first one has a trivial solution: an encoder that maps every field to the same constant vector makes the future perfectly predictable and drives the rollout error to zero while carrying no information about the flow. This is representational collapse, and it is a genuine attractor of the objective rather than a hypothetical. We suppress it with the characteristic-function regulariser of \citet{lejepa,lewm}, which matches the distribution of latents in a batch to an isotropic Gaussian along a small number of random projections; a constant encoder has zero variance along every projection, so the term penalises exactly the degenerate solution and keeps the latent covariance full rank. An automatic fall-back to a variance-covariance objective \citep{vicreg} is armed should the participation ratio of the latent indicate collapse regardless. Its configuration is given in appendix~\ref{app:reg}, and \S\ref{sec:res_construction} shows which supervision choices collapse in practice. A variant lineage, referred to below as the direct-forecaster-coupled lineage, replaces the autoregressive transformer in this rollout term with the direct multi-horizon forecaster of \S\ref{sec:methods_rex} as the training predictor, so that the encoder is shaped against a one-shot rather than a recursive forward model; it is compared at low dimension in \S\ref{sec:res_dimension}.\re

\rb{K}{S3-04}{Defines the three supervision heads and the wake descriptor. The five-citation provenance of the descriptor design could move to appendix A.}Three auxiliary heads map the latent state to aerodynamic observables during training. They exist because predictability alone does not fix what the state contains: the rollout term rewards any latent whose own evolution is easy to forecast, and the regulariser only keeps that latent from degenerating, so neither requires the state to carry the aerodynamic quantities a user of the model would want to read out of it. The heads supply that requirement directly, by making the load and the wake structure recoverable from $\zvec_t$ by a small map, and the conditioning study of \S\ref{sec:res_construction} is what establishes that they change the representation rather than merely decorating it. The three are

\setlength{\listtextleftmargin}{1.6em}\begin{enumerate}
  \item a lift head, mapping $\zvec_t$ to the instantaneous lift coefficient $C_L(t)$;
  \item a wake head, mapping $\zvec_t$ to an $80$-dimensional signed wake spectrum; and
  \item a near-body head, constructed in \S\ref{sec:methods_chang} below, mapping $\zvec_t$ to an $80$-dimensional lift-element observable of byte-matched capacity.
\end{enumerate}
All three read the encoder output, not the predictor output, so they constrain the state itself and not the forecast. All are trained with small weights jointly with the representation, none is part of the latent-prediction loss, and all are discarded once training ends. The scalar-lift lineage is the observable augmentation of \citet{fukami2023,fukami2025}; which head supplies which property of the state is the subject of the conditioning study of \S\ref{sec:res_construction}. The wake target itself is a fixed descriptor chosen by design rather than tuned: it pairs a coarse signed spatial pooling of the wake region, which retains the layout of the alternating positive and negative vortices while discarding pixel-level detail, the spatial-pyramid construction of \citet{lazebnik2006beyond}, with a radially-averaged power spectrum, the canonical isotropic scale descriptor of a turbulent field \citep{pope2001turbulent} and an established compact image descriptor in machine learning \citep{durall2020watch}, so it encodes both where the wake vortices sit and at which scales their energy resides. Pairing a localised spatial summary with a multiscale spectral one is the spatial-plus-scale representation formalised by the wavelet scattering transform \citep{brunamallat2013}, which has been applied to turbulent flow fields precisely to recover geometry that the power spectrum alone cannot resolve \citep{skinner2025characterizing}. We keep the descriptor compact, at $80$ dimensions, so that it constrains the wake structure without over-parameterising the target on the available training encounters. The head-supervision axis is no longer a confound: the $2 \times 2 \times 2$ conditioning cube of \S\ref{sec:res_construction}, three seeds per cell with matched reconstructive anchors, isolates each head's contribution under gates fixed in advance of evaluation.\re

\rb{A}{S3-05}{A five-point fairness enumeration whose content is stated again in S3-06, S3-11 and S31-01. Appendix A is where a controls-and-fairness ledger belongs; the body needs one sentence.}Comparing a lift observable against a wake observable is only meaningful if the comparison is fair, and five choices make it so. First, both field-observable targets are $80$-dimensional and byte-matched, built by the identical construction, so no head sees a richer training signal. Second, the heads share one network class and one loss weight, so the comparison isolates the supervision signal and not the head architecture. Third, the reconstructive baselines carry the identical heads, the AE-L, AE-W and AE-LW anchors of \S\ref{sec:res_construct}, so supervision is never an advantage of the predictive model alone and every supervised-versus-unsupervised and predictive-versus-reconstructive contrast is available separately. Fourth, the wake descriptor is assembled from established, fixed constructions, the spatial pyramid and the radial power spectrum above, and was not tuned against any outcome. Fifth, the probes are fit on the training split and scored on the held-out split alone. The one asymmetry a reader might raise, that the linear basis carries no supervision channel, is by design: supervision is a property of trainable encoders, so the proper orthogonal basis is the objective-free linear reference, read through the identical frozen probes, and the matched-supervision autoencoder exists precisely so that objective and supervision are separable.\re

\subsection{Matched-predictor closure protocol}
\label{sec:methods_closure}

\rb{K}{S3-06}{The single principle the comparison rests on: same pipeline, only the encoder differs. Also names the shared operator and the retired suited-operator protocol.}The comparison rests on a single principle: every baseline is the same pipeline, and only the encoder differs. Each encoder produces a latent trajectory $\zvec_{1:T}$ from the vorticity fields, a pooled coefficient vector at the same dimension for every family (the vector of proper-orthogonal temporal coefficients for the linear basis, the bottleneck vector for the autoencoders), and an identical probe family maps a latent to a physical observable for evaluation (figure~\ref{fig:eval_protocol}, appendix~\ref{app:reg}). The primary forecast-merit comparison of \S\ref{sec:res_forecast} realises the map of equation~\eqref{eq:forecast} with a single shared operator, the \DirectFC{} of \S\ref{sec:methods_rex}, trained identically on every family's latents, so the family ordering cannot be an artefact of operator choice. A legacy suited-operator protocol, a matched autoregressive transformer on the pooled states and a matched residual U-Net on the reference latents, each latent read under the operator numerically stable on it, is retained in appendix~\ref{app:reg} and the supplementary material, where the stability split itself is documented. The \PredState's own co-trained predictor, the co-trained reduced-order model, is reported alongside, and the sequential estimator of \S\ref{sec:methods_estimator} uses the same matched transformer on the \PredState's frozen trajectories. Every state is compared at the matched headline dimension $d = \LatentDim$. This isolation is the only fair way to attribute representation quality to the encoder rather than to incidental differences in the readout. The reconstructive references use their best-documented configuration, with ReLU activations and group normalisation rather than the strict published variant (tanh, no normalisation), which we found gives a worse parametric probe; the comparison is therefore against a tuned baseline, not a hobbled one. The observable heads are pinned to the same current-frame form for every family, so no family gains an accidental advantage from a differently-shaped supervision signal.\re

\rb{K}{S3-07}{Defines representational closure, the headline figure of merit, and scopes the rollout as secondary. The whole Results section is read through this definition.}The headline figure of merit is representational closure: a fixed linear probe is applied to the latent encoded directly from a held-out field, and we ask whether it recovers each of the five observables, the wake in particular. This isolates what the encoder carries, with no rollout. We report it as the held-out coefficient of determination $R^2$ on the \TestSplit{} and \BoundarySplit{} sets, with the per-observable mean absolute error alongside, and bootstrap $95\%$ confidence intervals over encounters. As a secondary, confirmatory measure we also report forecast closure: the predictor is rolled recursively from the pre-impact context to $H = 16$, and the same probe maps the predicted latent to each observable. A small forecast error means the encoder produces a latent whose dynamics, propagated by the predictor, retain the information the probe needs; a large error means either that the encoder discarded that information or that the predictor lost it during rollout, a distinction the drift diagnostic below resolves. The representational closure is in table~\ref{tab:closure}, which reports the readability and the matched-predictor forecast merit side by side. We do not headline a forecast-$R^2$-versus-horizon curve; the rollout is reported to show that the latent stays usable, not as a validated forecast.\re

\rb{D}{S3-08}{A two-sentence analogy between the filter's mixed timescales and the training objective. It carries no evidence and nothing downstream refers to it.}The mixed timescales of this estimator, a transition map trained on sequences and an emission map trained per instant, mirror the structure of the training objective in \S\ref{sec:methods_loss}: a dynamics equation is a statement about trajectories and an observation equation is a statement about single instants. The combined loss is a jointly trained state-space realisation, and the filter is the same decomposition read at inference.\re

\subsection{Diagnostics and controls}
\label{sec:methods_diag}

\rb{K}{S3-09}{Defines the latent-drift diagnostic used in table 8 and \S4.4. Without it the near-null decomposition cannot be read.}Two diagnostics turn the closure result into a mechanism. The first is the latent drift, which addresses the fact that a predictor with low one-step error need not produce a useful long rollout, because each prediction is fed back as the next input and small geometric errors accumulate. We quantify how far a rollout has left the training manifold by the Mahalanobis distance of the rolled-out latent at horizon $H$, measured against the distribution of simulation-encoded latents for the same encoder, normalised by the Mahalanobis distance of the simulation-encoded latent itself. A ratio near one means the rollout stays within the natural spread of the training latents; a large ratio means it has gone out of distribution, so that the probe is then queried outside its support.\re

\rb{K}{S3-10}{Defines the parameter-only floor that makes the wake result non-trivial. \S4.1 and \S5.1 both depend on it.}The second diagnostic is a parameter-only floor, which matters here because the model never sees the gust parameters: it bounds what the parameters alone could explain, so that any closure above it is genuinely supplied by the latent state. We compute it with a kernel-ridge regressor that maps $\cvec = (G,D,Y)$ directly to each observable at $H = 16$, with no latent input, fit on training and evaluated on the held-out splits. On wake enstrophy this floor is low, so the latent's wake closure is not something the parameters could have supplied. At the impact frame the same parameter floor is high, because the just-released gust nearly determines the instantaneous state; the gap opens as the wake evolves (\S\ref{sec:res_closure}).\re

\rb{K}{S3-11}{The one-factor-at-a-time controls and the objective-free supervised control. This is the paragraph that answers the confound objection.}Because the predictive encoder differs from the reconstructive baseline in objective, architecture, and auxiliary supervision at once, the comparison is accompanied by controls that vary one factor at a time (table~\ref{tab:baselines}): a crossing of objective with architecture at matched auxiliary heads, an ablation of each auxiliary head, and a conditioned predictor variant that adds $\cvec$ (\S\ref{sec:res_controls}). All the controls share the one encoder of table~\ref{tab:architecture} and differ only in what is trained on top of it. The objective-free supervised control isolates the objective most directly: it trains the shared encoder with the wake and lift heads and the anti-collapse term but with no predictive or reconstructive objective, no predictor and no decoder, so it holds the supervision fixed and removes every objective, isolating what the observable heads alone supply. The published reference recipes, a plain convolutional autoencoder in the Fukami--Taira lineage \citep{fukami2023,fukami2025}, the $\beta$-variational autoencoder of \citet{solera2024}, and a linear proper-orthogonal-decomposition basis, use their own native architectures but produce a pooled coefficient vector at the same latent dimension. Their forecast merit is refit under the legacy suited-operator protocol of \S\ref{sec:methods_closure}.\re
