Extreme vortex-gust encounters of a post-stall airfoil are wake-reorganisation
events: the load spike is the surface signature of leading-edge-vortex roll-up and
shedding, and the release parameters alone do not fix the wake after impact. Using
direct numerical simulations of a NACA~0012 airfoil at $\alpha = 14^\circ$ and
$\vRe = 5000$, perturbed by Taylor vortices spanning gust strength, core diameter
and wall-normal offset, we ask which reduced state preserves that wake. At fixed
dimension, under one shared probe and predictor, we compare a wake-supervised
predictive state, a lift-augmented reconstructive autoencoder (Fukami-Taira
lineage), and a proper orthogonal decomposition basis; matched
controls separate objective from supervision.
Probed from held-out fields sixteen frames after impact, the wake-supervised state
reads the wake enstrophy ($R^2 = \NumReprWakeJepaTf$) where the no-wake baselines
are at or below the mean predictor; but an objective-free supervised encoder reads
it equally well, and a matched reconstructive control reads it comparably under
case-disjoint retraining, so the wake supervision, not the objective, supplies the
readability. The predictive objective is distinguished dynamically: it gives the
most accurate wake rollout, reported as secondary.
The mechanism is geometric: the anti-collapse regulariser keeps the predictive
and supervised rollouts on the encoded manifold
while the reconstructive rollout departs along low-variance
directions, and the predictive no-gust shedding orbit stays a single loop
where the reconstructive one fragments. Sparse wall pressure recovers the predictive
state best from a strictly pre-impact window, a state-recovery property rather than
direct wake recovery.
